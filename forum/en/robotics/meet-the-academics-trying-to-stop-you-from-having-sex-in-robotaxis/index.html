Academics Explore Design Solutions to Deter Robotaxi Misuse

A team of researchers is gathering at an industry conference to address how physical and design-based interventions could prevent passengers from engaging in illicit activities within autonomous vehicles. By treating the vehicle cabin as a communal space, they hope to preserve the reputation of emerging transport technology.

Radwan AbdulhadiFounder and editor, RDWAN Tech
Published: September 20, 2026
Updated: September 20, 2026 · 4 min read

Key points

  • Researchers are hosting a workshop in Sweden to explore how design can discourage inappropriate passenger behavior in self-driving cars.
  • The initiative aims to prevent 'antisocial' acts that could damage public perception of autonomous transit technologies.
  • Experts are considering features like increased transparency and sensor-based alerts to maintain social norms.
  • The findings are intended to inform future safety standards for shared autonomous transport systems.

The Challenge of Unsupervised Autonomous Transit

As the deployment of autonomous vehicles expands, researchers are increasingly concerned about how these spaces will be utilized in the absence of a human driver. Pavlo Bazilinskyy, an assistant professor at the Eindhoven University of Technology, has spent over a decade researching human-robot interaction. His recent investigations into how individuals behave in transport environments have highlighted a significant, under-studied gap in the industry. With Robotaxis without cabbies behind the wheel now operating in various regions, the lack of an authority figure inside the cabin creates an environment where traditional social norms may be disregarded.

The concerns mirror historical issues observed in other modes of transport, including trains and airplanes. However, the unique, quasi-private nature of a robotaxi presents a new set of challenges for designers. The goal of this academic inquiry is not merely to police behavior for moral reasons, but to address what happens when private vehicles transition into mass-market public transport solutions. Understanding these risks is part of a larger effort to ensure that the future of mobility remains orderly and safe for all demographics.

An Academic Focus on Preventive Design

This week, a group of specialists—including systems engineers, computer scientists, and psychologists—are meeting at an industry conference in Gothenburg, Sweden, to hold a workshop dedicated to this topic. The event serves as a platform to discuss how the architecture of a vehicle’s interior might influence passenger conduct. According to Alexandros Rouchitsas, an engineering psychologist and co-organizer of the workshop, the research team is acting in the interest of the general public, including the elderly and children, who stand to benefit most from autonomous travel options.

The primary concern is that a pattern of 'antisocial' behavior could lead to a negative public image for what is otherwise a potentially positive technology. Rouchitsas and his peers argue that for autonomous systems to be successfully adopted, they must be viewed as equitable, serious, and societally minded. They are particularly wary of the technology becoming associated with hedonistic misuse, which could undermine trust and limit accessibility for those who rely on these services for essential mobility.

Developing Physical and Technological Interventions

During the four-hour workshop, participants are tasked with brainstorming concrete interventions. These may include structural changes or software-based alerts. Rouchitsas suggested several preliminary concepts, such as increasing the transparency of the cabin to deter unwanted acts or implementing light-based systems that activate when sensors detect disapproved conduct. While these ideas are in the early stages of development, the organizers believe that establishing a baseline for how machines detect and respond to human behavior is critical for the long-term success of the sector.

The organizers intend to synthesize the contributions from this workshop into a formal academic paper. This document will be intended for use by designers, automotive companies, and lawmakers as they establish regulations for future transportation systems. By effectively addressing consensual interactions, the researchers hope to create a framework that can also be applied to preventing more severe incidents, such as bullying, robbery, or violence, within shared autonomous spaces.

Broader Implications for Mobility Infrastructure

The location of the workshop, Gothenburg, is a hub for automotive innovation, counting industry giants like Volvo and technology providers like SmartEye among its local entities. With sponsors such as BMW and Google, the conference attracts a diverse mix of academic and industry voices. This collaboration is essential, as the implementation of these safety measures will ultimately fall to the manufacturers and software developers who build the vehicles of the future.

As these technologies evolve, the focus extends to ensuring that all segments of the population—including people with disabilities who may require specialized accessibility—can utilize these systems without fear. The academic consensus remains that if the industry ignores these societal challenges, the adoption of autonomous, communal transport could be hindered by public distrust. By tackling the 'elephant in the room' now, researchers hope to set a precedent for ethical and functional design in the next generation of transportation.
