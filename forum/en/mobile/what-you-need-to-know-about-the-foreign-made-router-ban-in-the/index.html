FCC Restrictions on Foreign-Made Consumer Routers Explained

The Federal Communications Commission has introduced new regulatory hurdles for consumer-grade networking equipment manufactured outside the United States. While existing devices remain unaffected, new market entries now require conditional authorization.

Radwan AbdulhadiFounder and editor, RDWAN Tech
Published: September 20, 2026
Updated: September 20, 2026 · 4 min read

Key points

  • The FCC has restricted the sale of new foreign-made consumer routers and mobile hot spots to address national security risks.
  • Existing routers currently in use or already on sale in the US are exempt from the ban and will continue to receive support until 2029.
  • Manufacturers can apply for Conditional Approval by disclosing supply chains and committing to US-based manufacturing plans.
  • Companies including Netgear, Asus, and Amazon’s Eero have secured conditional waivers to continue operations.

Understanding the FCC Regulatory Action

In March, the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) initiated a ban on the sale of new consumer-grade Wi-Fi routers and mobile hot spots manufactured outside the United States. According to the regulatory body, this decision is rooted in national security concerns, as foreign-made hardware has previously been linked to large-scale cyberattacks. The official statement from the agency noted that foreign-made routers were involved in the Volt, Flax, and Salt Typhoon cyberattacks, which targeted vital US infrastructure. As the FCC wrote, these devices have been exploited to facilitate espionage, intellectual property theft, and network disruption.

Scope of the Equipment Ban

The restriction specifically targets new hardware imports and sales. It does not apply to any equipment already deployed in American households, nor does it affect devices currently available for retail purchase that have already received FCC approval. Furthermore, the ruling does not extend to consumer smartphones that include integrated hotspot capabilities. For those concerned about their current home networks, there is no immediate requirement to replace existing infrastructure, as confirmed by the FCC’s FAQ. These units are permitted to remain in use and continue receiving firmware updates until at least January 1, 2029.

Conditional Approval Pathways

While the mandate imposes strict barriers, the government has established a process for manufacturers to continue operations through a system of Conditional Approval. To qualify, companies must comply with three rigorous requirements: detailed disclosure of corporate ownership and foreign government ties; comprehensive supply chain mapping that identifies the origin of every component; and a transparent, time-bound roadmap for establishing or expanding manufacturing capacity within the United States. These guidelines are intended to provide the Department of Defense and the Department of Homeland Security with the oversight necessary to mitigate potential threats.

Several industry leaders have already navigated this application process successfully. As of September 2026, companies including Netgear, Amazon’s Eero, and Asus have been granted conditional waivers. These approvals allow the firms to continue releasing new hardware, including mesh systems, for a limited 18-month window. Other entities, such as Arcadyan—a key supplier for major telecommunications carriers—have also received similar authorization.

Industry Implications and Security Concerns

The broader cybersecurity community has weighed in on the necessity of these measures. Bogdan Botezatu, director of Threat Research at the cybersecurity firm Bitdefender, characterizes the ban as a logical step in hardening domestic infrastructure. He emphasizes that consumer routers are often the most vulnerable perimeter of a home network, making them strategic targets for malicious actors. This initiative follows a pattern of recent government interventions in the tech hardware space, including a recent federal drone ban and a recent robot vacuum ban, both of which aim to reduce reliance on foreign-produced technology in sensitive settings.

Maintaining Long-Term Security

One primary concern raised by the Consumer Technology Association (CTA) and security experts is the potential for orphan hardware. There is a risk that if manufacturers fail to secure approvals, devices might be cut off from essential security patches. Experts like Matt Wyckhouse, CEO of Finite State, have warned that leaving millions of routers without software support could create significant long-term vulnerabilities. Recognizing this, the FCC has extended the waiver for software and firmware updates until January 1, 2029, ensuring that deployed devices remain protected while the market transitions to the new regulatory environment. Consumers currently shopping for hardware should check the manufacturer’s status and remain aware that while many high-quality options exist, the market is undergoing a period of structural change.
