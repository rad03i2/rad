AI Industry Divided Over Future of Regulatory Frameworks

A push for industry-wide AI safety standards faces significant internal resistance and skepticism from the White House, leaving the future of AI governance in flux.

Radwan AbdulhadiFounder and editor, RDWAN Tech
Published: September 21, 2026
Updated: September 21, 2026 · 3 min read

Key points

  • Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei proposed a three-step safety plan including third-party oversight, drawing mixed reactions from industry peers.
  • Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg and other tech leaders have pushed back against industry-funded regulatory models, prioritizing company autonomy.
  • The Trump administration has labeled concerns over AI safety as a 'hoax,' complicating efforts to establish formal government oversight.
  • Experts argue that current regulatory framing focuses too heavily on existential risks while ignoring immediate, systemic safety concerns.

The Push for Oversight

The artificial intelligence landscape is currently defined by a high-stakes standoff between industry titans. Earlier this week, the sector seemed to gravitate toward a consensus on regulation. Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei proposed a comprehensive three-step plan aimed at slowing development, which involves embedding third-party evaluators within laboratories, fostering domestic industry coordination, and pursuing international agreements. Major figures, including OpenAI CEO Sam Altman and Google DeepMind co-founder Demis Hassabis, appeared to align with elements of this proposal, though the reality of implementation remains complex as these companies are [dropping] hints about ongoing work toward an internal framework.

Internal Industry Disagreements

Despite the tentative support for safety initiatives, a unified front has failed to materialize. Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg has vocalized opposition to restricting corporate autonomy, arguing that labs should maintain the flexibility to train models safely on their own terms. Reports indicate that Zuckerberg, along with Elon Musk and Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang, were instrumental in scuttling plans for an independent, industry-funded regulator modeled after the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority. This pushback highlights a broader philosophical split regarding how much control firms should cede to external bodies, even as some companies continue to report [concerning] safety incidents under their internal protocols.

White House Stance and Regulatory 'Vibes'

The political environment in Washington has proven equally unpredictable. The Trump administration has openly criticized the notion of an AI safety crisis, with the President publicly dismissing such fears as a 'hoax.' In a recent instance, President Trump [called Huang] during an industry conference, emphasizing his belief that robots would not be taking over. This rhetoric marks a shift from his first term, during which he signed Executive Order 13960 to address privacy and civil rights in federal AI usage. While some observers, such as blogger Zvi Mowshowitz, suggest that Trump’s approach is driven more by 'vibes' than consistent policy, the administration’s skepticism toward existential risk narratives has created a challenging environment for proponents of formal federal oversight.

Safety as a Systemic Necessity

Proponents of regulation, such as Nick Reese, a former Department of Homeland Security official, argue that the current debate is fundamentally flawed. Reese notes that by focusing exclusively on 'doomsday' scenarios, the industry misses the more immediate, tangible risks posed by current technology. He suggests that the industry should move toward a model of mandatory national safety standards, similar to those found in the aviation or financial sectors, where the cost of systemic failure is too high to accept. According to Reese, the resistance to such standards rests on a 'tired' belief that regulation is inherently detrimental to innovation, ignoring the historical precedent where safety standards have ultimately bolstered the widespread adoption of new technologies.

Future Outlook

OpenAI remains a key advocate for government involvement. Chris Lehane, the company's global affairs chief, emphasized that while safety begins with internal corporate steps, there is a clear role for government policy. OpenAI is actively calling for Congress to capitalize on bipartisan interest to create a framework that balances innovation with mandatory safety requirements. While companies like Anthropic have expressed commitment to navigating this regulatory landscape despite accusations of 'doomerism,' the divide between firms prioritizing autonomy and those seeking structural oversight ensures that the debate remains a central theme in the future of the technology sector, as explored in recent coverage of [The AI regulation smackdown isn’t over].
