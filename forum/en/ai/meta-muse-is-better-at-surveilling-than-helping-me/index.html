Meta Launches Muse AI Agent Amid Data Privacy Concerns

Meta has introduced its personal AI agent, Muse, designed to manage daily tasks through web automation. While the tool demonstrates significant browsing capabilities, it faces scrutiny over its data-gathering practices and default settings.

Radwan AbdulhadiFounder and editor, RDWAN Tech
Published: September 20, 2026
Updated: September 20, 2026 · 3 min read

Key points

  • Meta’s Muse app has surpassed 900,000 downloads in its first week of availability.
  • The AI agent automates tasks like booking reservations and managing inboxes by using a virtual machine to interact with websites.
  • Privacy advocates have criticized the app for automatically opting users into AI model training.
  • Meta maintains that data collection is essential for improving model performance and personalizing the user experience.

The Rise of Muse

Meta has officially entered the competitive space of personal AI assistants with the release of Muse, an application that allows users to offload daily responsibilities such as scheduling and information retrieval. The app, which has been heavily promoted across other platforms like Instagram, acts as an agent that executes tasks by browsing the web through a virtual machine. This approach differs from the traditional interface of a standard chatbot, as users interact with the agent via a messaging-style interface.

Functionality and Market Performance

According to data from Sensor Tower, the tool has seen rapid adoption, reaching over 900,000 downloads within its first week. Meta has positioned Muse as a versatile helper capable of managing complex operations, such as navigating e-commerce sites or messaging sellers on Facebook Marketplace. Unlike earlier iterations of such technology, Muse reportedly maintains high accuracy when browsing the internet, allowing it to complete specific user requests like ordering food or comparing local deals.

Data Privacy and Collection Concerns

Despite its functional utility, the app has drawn criticism for its data-heavy design. The application frequently prompts users to integrate sensitive information, including email access, bank account details, and even identification documents. Critics, including those from the Electronic Frontier Foundation, note that users often mistake these AI interfaces for neutral tools, failing to realize they are feeding information directly into corporate servers. Furthermore, users are automatically opted into a program that uses their interactions to train future AI models, a practice that requires manual intervention to disable.

Corporate Perspective and Future Security

Meta spokesperson Emil Vazquez has defended the app, asserting that Muse is built with robust protections and user controls. Tarek Sheasha, vice president at Meta Superintelligence Labs, noted in a blog post that the current data collection process is a necessary default to improve the model's understanding of human life. The company has stated that it plans to roll out more secure, confidential versions of its virtual machine later this year, which are intended to prevent the company from accessing user data through cryptographic means.

The Broader Landscape of AI Agents

The release of Muse marks Meta’s entry into a crowded field of AI agents, joining competitors like Instinct and OpenClaw that are gaining traction in Silicon Valley. These tools are characterized by their ambition to move beyond simple Q&A functions to active, task-oriented behavior. As these AI agents become more deeply integrated into daily workflows through platforms like WhatsApp, the debate surrounding user trust, data ownership, and the implications of automated surveillance is likely to intensify.
